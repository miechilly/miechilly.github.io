Project overview

Research on cement concrete containing high replacement levels of Class C and Class F fly ash. The study investigates whether industrial byproducts can reduce reliance on Portland cement while preserving performance relevant to infrastructure applications.

Research methods

  • Reviewed concrete mixture proportions and experimental data.
  • Examined workability using slump testing and mechanical performance using compressive strength tests.
  • Analyzed electrical resistivity as a durability-related indicator across curing ages.
  • Prepared technical presentation materials for the Kennesaw State University Undergraduate Research Symposium.

What the results help explain

Comparing strength, workability, and resistivity helps reveal trade-offs in high-volume fly ash substitution. See the original poster for numerical findings, conditions, and limitations.

01 / Why this research matters

Can concrete use substantially less Portland cement?

Portland cement production contributes significantly to concrete's embodied carbon. Class C and Class F fly ash are coal-combustion byproducts that can replace a portion of cementitious material, but their different chemistry and reactivity can change early-age strength, later-age performance, workability and transport properties. The research question is not simply whether more cement can be replaced; it is whether a resulting mixture remains useful for engineering applications.

Class C fly ash generally has more calcium and may contribute to cementitious reactions; Class F fly ash is generally lower in calcium and relies more heavily on pozzolanic reactions. These differences are why the two classes should be evaluated separately instead of treated as interchangeable materials.

02 / Experimental workflow

From mixture design to hardened concrete

1. Design and batching

Establish a conventional concrete control and fly-ash substitution mixtures. Keep track of the cementitious proportions, aggregate quantities and water content so that differences in measured properties can be interpreted in relation to the mixtures.

2. Fresh-concrete testing

Measure slump to characterize consistency and workability before casting. Slump is useful for identifying changes in how the material handles, but it is not a direct measure of strength or durability.

3. Specimen preparation and curing

Cast concrete cylinders and maintain consistent specimen identification and curing conditions. Consistent handling is essential because curing and testing age can affect the measured results.

4. Strength and resistivity

Use compression tests to characterize load-carrying performance and surface electrical resistivity to provide an indicator related to ionic transport and potential resistance to chloride penetration. Interpret resistivity alongside mixture age and moisture condition.

03 / Interpreting the results

Strength, durability and sustainability are different questions

Higher fly ash substitution may lower Portland cement demand, but a lower cement fraction does not automatically yield a suitable structural mix. Compression testing asks whether the concrete can resist applied stress. Surface resistivity offers a separate perspective on transport-related durability. Slump indicates workability during placement. An engineering recommendation should consider all three rather than selecting a mix based on a single favorable measurement.

Compressive strength

Compare tested cylinders at matched curing ages against the control. Any apparent improvement or loss must be interpreted in the context of the target design strength and intended application.

Surface resistivity

Compare resistivity at consistent ages and test conditions. Higher values can suggest reduced ionic mobility, but resistivity alone does not establish long-term field service life.

04 / Practical application and limitations

What this means for infrastructure

High-volume fly ash mixtures are relevant to sustainable pavements, foundations and other concrete elements where reducing cement demand is valuable. Practical use still requires adequate strength development, reliable placement characteristics, and durability appropriate to exposure conditions. Findings from laboratory specimens cannot be transferred directly to every field application without further qualification.

Limitations to consider: differences in fly ash source and chemistry, curing duration, mixture proportions, test variability, and the gap between laboratory indicators and long-term field performance. Future analysis could compare mixtures against a specified strength threshold and quantify environmental impact per unit of delivered performance.

05 / Experimental design and measured findings

The study in numbers

The shared experimental program compared fifteen concrete mixtures: a Portland-cement control and seven replacement levels for each of the two fly ash classes. Replacement levels ranged from 10% to 90% by weight. The reported water-to-cementitious-material ratio was held at 0.45, and aggregate and water quantities were kept constant so fly ash content could be investigated as the principal variable.

06 / Laboratory standards and interpretation

What each test tells an engineer

ASTM C143 — slump

Fresh concrete is placed into the slump cone in layers, consolidated, and the mold is lifted vertically. The measured settlement provides a consistency indicator. It helps compare mixtures during batching and casting but should not be used as a substitute for mechanical performance testing.

ASTM C39 — compressive strength

Cured cylinders are loaded in compression until failure. Maximum load divided by the specimen's cross-sectional area gives compressive stress. Comparable specimen geometry, curing age, end preparation, and loading procedures are important for fair comparisons.

AASHTO T358 — surface resistivity

A four-point Wenner probe provides an electrical resistivity measurement on the concrete surface. Higher readings can indicate lower ionic conductivity and potentially better resistance to chloride transport, though moisture and curing conditions influence the reading.

Quality control and traceability

Mix identifiers, batching records, test ages, replicate cylinders, and laboratory worksheets connect each reported number to a physical specimen. Maintaining that traceability matters when interpreting differences among replacement levels.

Where the approach reaches its limit

At 90% replacement, both fly ash classes exhibited major strength losses in the reported program. The 50% Class C recommendation is also qualified by missing 28-day data for the 70% Class C mixture following laboratory maintenance. The most promising tested mix is therefore not proof that every higher replacement level will fail or succeed.
