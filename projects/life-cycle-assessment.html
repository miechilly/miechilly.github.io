Life Cycle Assessment of Cement Concrete Containing Industrial Waste Materials

Evaluating environmental impacts and performance trade-offs in concrete with industrial byproducts.

← All research projects

Project overview

This research examines environmental and material-performance trade-offs for cement concrete containing industrial waste materials, including fly ash, recycled glass, crumb rubber, and steel slag.

Research approach

  • Compared concrete material alternatives from a life-cycle assessment perspective.
  • Considered global warming potential alongside compressive strength and electrical resistivity.
  • Analyzed and communicated the relationship between environmental impacts and material performance.
  • Presented the work at the Kennesaw State University Undergraduate Research Symposium.

Engineering significance

Lower embodied environmental impact alone does not establish a suitable construction material. A meaningful comparison considers functional performance and how material selection affects long-term infrastructure outcomes. Refer to the research poster for study-specific results.

01 / Research motivation

Lower-carbon concrete must still perform

Cement is an important contributor to the environmental footprint of conventional concrete. Replacing some cement with industrial byproducts can reduce demand for virgin cement, but a mixture's environmental advantage has to be evaluated alongside its engineering performance. A material that has low emissions but fails to meet service requirements is not automatically the most sustainable choice.

The project examines cement concrete containing industrial waste-derived materials, including fly ash, recycled glass, crumb rubber and steel slag. These materials differ in how they are produced, processed and used, so each alternative needs a clearly defined comparison basis.

02 / How an LCA works

Define the system before comparing carbon footprints

Functional comparison

Specify what is being compared, such as one unit volume of concrete. For design decisions, also consider whether the mixtures meet equivalent strength and durability requirements.

System boundary

Identify which stages are included: raw material production, processing, transport, batching, placement, service life and end-of-life. Results can change when the boundary changes.

Inventory inputs

Account for cement, aggregates, replacement materials, water and relevant energy use. Document assumptions about byproduct processing and the allocation of environmental burdens.

Impact interpretation

Calculate an environmental indicator such as global warming potential (GWP), then examine how sensitive the comparison is to assumptions and mixture performance.

Scope note: The research poster is the authoritative source for this study's specific modeled system boundary, inventory and results. The workflow above explains the LCA concepts without claiming an unverified software configuration or inventory database.

03 / Reading the analysis

Embodied impact versus engineering usefulness

Comparing concrete mixtures by environmental impact alone can reward very high replacement rates even if the material becomes too weak for its intended use. A more meaningful decision examines impact and performance together. For example, two mixtures with similar GWP may differ in compressive strength, resistivity, curing needs or likely service life.

Environmental side

What environmental burdens are avoided by replacing cement? What additional processing or transport does the replacement material require? How sensitive is the result to these assumptions?

Engineering side

Does the alternative mix meet its intended strength requirement? What do workability and durability indicators suggest? Can the comparison be made on an equivalent-performance basis?

04 / Research interpretation

Why the study matters

Life cycle assessment can help engineers distinguish a material that is merely lower-carbon per cubic meter from one that offers a credible combination of environmental benefit and structural utility. This is relevant to transportation infrastructure, pavement materials and other concrete-intensive projects where material selection affects both construction impacts and long-term performance.

Uncertainty and limitations

Important uncertainties include geographic differences in electricity and material supply, transportation distance, allocation methods for industrial byproducts, the representativeness of test specimens, and whether the modeled boundary captures use-phase and end-of-life effects. Environmental conclusions should always be read in light of these choices.

05 / Scope of the assessment

How we accounted for embodied carbon

Our life-cycle assessment examined concrete containing industrial waste-derived cement replacement materials. The study used a process-based, cradle-to-gate framework in openLCA 2.6.0 with the TRACI 2.1 impact assessment method and U.S. Life Cycle Inventory data. The modeled boundary covered upstream raw-material production and batching electricity; it did not include transportation to the construction site, construction activities, service life, or end-of-life treatment.

The broader comparison covered eleven cement replacement materials. Recycled glass, crumb rubber, and steel slag were represented using data from previous experiments rather than concrete batches that I personally created. Keeping that distinction clear helps separate the team's direct laboratory measurements from secondary inventory inputs.

Goal and scope

Ask whether reducing Portland cement content can lower global warming potential while maintaining useful concrete performance. Define a comparable concrete volume and record what activities are inside and outside the assessment.

Life-cycle inventory

Track material quantities from the mix-design worksheets, connect them to emissions data, and include modeled batching electricity. Material production assumptions can strongly influence the final estimate.

Impact calculation

Convert the inventory into global warming potential, reported as kilograms of carbon-dioxide equivalent per cubic meter of concrete (kg CO₂-eq/m³). This metric summarizes climate impact within the selected boundary.

Performance comparison

Interpret carbon results alongside 28-day compressive strength and surface resistivity. A low-carbon mixture that cannot meet its intended structural requirements is not an adequate engineering recommendation.

The 50% Class C mixture stood out because it paired a large modeled carbon reduction with strength and resistivity readings above the control. This does not mean 50% replacement is optimal in every setting; fly ash source, available materials, strength specifications, and local emissions inventories all matter.

Why the performance index matters

Ranking mixtures by global warming potential alone can favor mixes with unacceptable mechanical performance. In the shared research, a performance index normalized carbon impact against strength and durability relative to the control, allowing the team to compare mixtures on more than one criterion. The 50% Class C mix ranked highest across the five strength-versus-durability weightings evaluated. Such indices support decisions, but the chosen weighting is an engineering judgment, not a substitute for minimum design requirements.

07 / Engineering interpretation

How these findings translate to infrastructure decisions

A practical screening process first verifies that a mixture meets the required strength and applicable durability criteria, then compares embodied carbon among the qualifying alternatives. The shared study considered 20.7 MPa (3,000 psi) and 34.5 MPa (5,000 psi) as illustrative strength thresholds. The reported results indicate that both fly ash classes met the lower threshold through 50% replacement; at the higher threshold, Class C met it through 50% and Class F through 30%.

These thresholds illustrate how the answer changes with application. A mixture considered for one nonstructural use may not satisfy a more demanding structural element. Additional qualification would be needed for exposure classes, long-term field durability, constructability, and project-specific codes.

Sources of uncertainty

Cradle-to-gate modeling omits transport, use-phase maintenance, and end-of-life impacts. Inventory datasets may not match the exact source or production process of local materials. Laboratory strengths and resistivity values also have their own variability, while comparisons between measured mixtures and previously published material data may not be perfectly equivalent.
