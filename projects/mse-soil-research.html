Project overview

Laboratory characterization of soil and aggregate materials being investigated for use as backfill in mechanically stabilized earth (MSE) walls. The work focuses on gradation and hydraulic conductivity, two properties relevant to drainage and geotechnical design.

Laboratory methodology

  1. Gradation review: compare sieve-analysis percent-finer data for Norcross, Ellijay, Friendship, and three Luckstone materials.
  2. Specimen preparation: calculate soil mass from target dry density and chamber volume; divide material into lifts for placement.
  3. Permeability testing: saturate the prepared specimen, record collected water volume, elapsed time, and head loss using the constant-head apparatus.
  4. Data analysis: compute and compare hydraulic conductivity across repeated trials in Excel.

01 / Engineering problem

Why MSE wall backfill properties matter

Mechanically stabilized earth (MSE) walls use reinforced soil to support roadways and other infrastructure. The backfill is a major part of the system: its particle-size distribution, compaction and ability to transmit water influence construction behavior and drainage. A higher percentage of fine particles may reduce permeability, making gradation and hydraulic conductivity important to characterize before drawing conclusions about material suitability.

This laboratory work compares several named aggregate sources: Norcross, Ellijay, Friendship, and three Luckstone samples. The supplied worksheets document gradation and constant-head permeability measurements.

03 / Laboratory procedure

Constant-head permeability testing

1. Prepare the specimen

Measure the available chamber dimensions, determine the required soil mass from the target dry unit weight, and place material in controlled lifts.

2. Saturate the soil

Establish water flow through the specimen and minimize trapped air, which can affect measured discharge and hydraulic conductivity.

3. Measure flow and head

Record collected discharge volume, elapsed time, head loss, specimen length and cross-sectional area during repeated trials.

4. Calculate conductivity

Apply Darcy's law to the measured values, then compare trials and material averages. Repeatability and confirmed specimen geometry are essential for reliable results.

Darcy's law for a constant-head test

k = (Q × L) / (A × h × t)

Here Q is collected water volume, L is specimen length, A is specimen area, h is head loss and t is elapsed time.

05 / Interpretation and next steps

From lab measurements to wall performance

Permeability testing is one part of evaluating backfill for an MSE wall. A complete assessment also considers material specifications, compaction, drainage design, reinforcement interaction and the wall's service conditions. These laboratory measurements can help identify which sources warrant closer study, but they should not be used alone to declare a material acceptable or unacceptable.

Future research

Confirm specimen dimensions and units, finish the remaining material trials, check repeatability, plot full gradation curves, and evaluate relationships between fines content and measured hydraulic conductivity. If GeoStudio modeling is undertaken, measured properties can inform a sensitivity analysis of water movement and wall behavior; that modeling is described here as future work, not a completed result.

05 / Why these measurements matter

Connecting gradation, compaction and drainage

Mechanically stabilized earth (MSE) walls rely on engineered backfill interacting with reinforcement. Water movement through that backfill is an important design consideration because persistent water can change drainage conditions and influence pore-water pressures. Laboratory gradation and permeability tests provide two complementary descriptions of the material: what particle sizes it contains and how readily water passes through a prepared specimen.

Particle-size distribution

Sieve analysis separates a dried soil sample through a stack of increasingly fine openings. The mass retained on each sieve is used to calculate percent retained, cumulative retained, and percent passing. A gradation curve summarizes the distribution and supports comparison among candidate materials.

Hydraulic conductivity

A constant-head test passes water through a saturated specimen under a measured head difference. The discharge collected over a known interval, specimen dimensions, and hydraulic gradient are combined to estimate conductivity. The result describes the tested specimen under laboratory conditions.

Specimen preparation

Specimen dimensions, mass, density, and preparation technique influence the void structure. Two samples of the same material can yield different flow behavior if they are compacted or saturated differently, so careful records are essential.

Engineering judgment

Neither a gradation curve nor a single conductivity value establishes MSE wall suitability by itself. Specifications, reinforcement compatibility, durability, drainage detailing, and project-specific material requirements also need to be evaluated.

06 / Calculation workflow

Turning measurements into hydraulic conductivity

For a constant-head permeability test, Darcy's law relates volumetric flow rate to the cross-sectional area and hydraulic gradient. With the measured collected volume V over elapsed time t, specimen length L, specimen area A, and head difference Δh, the laboratory estimate is:

k = (V × L) / (A × t × Δh)

Each variable must use consistent units. For example, using centimeters and seconds gives a conductivity in cm/s; the value can then be converted to m/s for reporting. The hydraulic gradient is Δh/L, while the flow rate is V/t. These quantities are conceptually different, even though both appear in the calculation.

How I carried out the laboratory workflow

  1. Identify and characterize: label material samples, run the sieve stack, and record retained masses for a gradation comparison.
  2. Prepare the specimen: document dimensions and mass, place material in the chamber, and record preparation conditions.
  3. Saturate: introduce water and allow trapped air to escape, following the laboratory procedure, before treating the discharge as steady-state flow.
  4. Measure: record the applied head, collection interval, and discharge volume for each trial.
  5. Calculate and review: compute hydraulic conductivity, check units and outliers, and compare repeated observations before interpreting differences between materials.

07 / Interpreting preliminary data

What the tests can—and cannot—show

Comparing preliminary hydraulic conductivity measurements can reveal which prepared specimens transmitted water more readily under the laboratory head and packing conditions. A relatively coarse gradation may suggest larger interconnected voids, but actual conductivity also depends on fines content, particle shape, density, segregation, saturation, and the test setup. Gradation alone should therefore not be used to assign a conductivity value.

Before drawing a design conclusion, I would check the consistency of replicate measurements, whether a stable head and flow rate were maintained, whether air remained in the specimen, and whether the material was prepared comparably across tests. If any of these conditions differ, an apparent difference between samples may reflect the procedure as well as the material.

How this supports MSE wall research

Well-documented hydraulic and gradation properties provide inputs for selecting backfill materials, evaluating drainage behavior, and identifying where additional testing or modeling is warranted. They are part of a broader geotechnical evaluation—not a direct prediction of wall deformation or stability.
